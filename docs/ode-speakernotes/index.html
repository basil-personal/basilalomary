Slide 01 / 12 · The thesis

AI Transformation at Jersey Mike’s

Open faster, recover the investment sooner, and improve unit economics.

Today I’m going to walk through an AI transformation thesis for Jersey Mike’s, starting with the period between signing a lease and opening the doors.

My hypothesis is that better coordination during that period can help franchisees start earning revenue earlier, reduce avoidable costs, and improve their investment payback. For Jersey Mike’s, it can also accelerate royalty receipts across the system.

I’ll cover the opportunity, how I would test it with the CEO and operating team, and what we could build in the first 90 days. This is a proposed transformation, so the assessment is designed to establish which parts of the hypothesis actually hold up.

Slide 02 / 12 · The opportunity

Several opportunities to leverage AI across the value chain

Start with a workflow where AI can affect a measurable business outcome.

There are opportunities across the entire value chain, from recruiting franchisees and choosing locations to running stores and supporting owners. I wouldn’t try to pursue all of them at once.

The starting point I’ve selected is permitting, build-out, and opening readiness. These activities bring together documents, drawings, comments, schedules, and a lot of communication across organizations. That makes them a plausible fit for AI that can interpret information and help people act on it.

The incentive is shared. Franchisees benefit from earlier sales and potentially lower costs. Jersey Mike’s receives a 6.5% royalty on sales, and the system receives a 5% marketing contribution. Those are different economic streams: the marketing contribution is not the same as corporate profit.

The question is whether the document and coordination work is actually holding back openings. That is what I would validate first.

Slide 03 / 12 · Where to start

Start with opening execution, where delays are measurable and earlier sales have clear value

Find the delays that actually move opening day.

The deck cites 130 gross openings in the first half of 2026 and more than 1,250 signed stores in the multi-year pipeline. That suggests a repeated workflow where even a modest improvement could matter across many projects. The signed pipeline is not a forecast of openings in a single year.

From lease signing to launch, a store moves through planning, design, permitting, construction, staffing, and inspections. But these stages don’t happen neatly in sequence. Procurement, utilities, and recruiting run alongside the physical build.

That means the real question is what sits on the critical path. An unanswered contractor question or a missing submission can delay downstream work. Other delays, such as a city’s review queue, may be largely outside our control.

With more than 90% of the development pipeline coming from existing franchisees, I’d also want to learn from experienced operators: where do they still get stuck, and what have the best ones already solved?

Slide 04 / 12 · The operating system

An opening operating system can coordinate every step from lease signing to launch

Turn scattered updates into clear owners, decisions, and next actions.

The vision is one opening plan that franchisees, architects, contractors, and corporate teams can work from. It should show what is complete, what is blocked, who owns the next decision, and what that means for the opening date.

Take a contractor waiting on a design decision. The system could find the relevant approved drawing and email history, prepare a response, and route it to the right person. Or take a permit package: it could check the required documents, flag missing information, and help prepare the response to reviewer comments.

The value comes from moving the work forward. A summary is useful only if it helps resolve the blockage.

People would still own design decisions, regulatory submissions, and commitments that affect cost or timing. We would track outcomes such as resubmissions, time awaiting decisions, blocked construction days, and readiness delays to see whether the system is helping.

Slide 05 / 12 · The economic case

Earlier openings generate sales for franchisees and fee revenue for Jersey Mike’s

Use the model as a sensitivity, not a promised result.

This is an illustration, not a forecast of what AI will deliver. The default scenario assumes 250 eligible stores open four months earlier and generate sales at 75% of the reported $1.376 million annual unit volume.

Under those assumptions, the model shows $86 million of system sales during the accelerated months, or $344,000 per store. That corresponds to approximately $5.6 million in royalty receipts and $4.3 million in marketing contributions.

The more useful anchor may be the one-month sensitivity: roughly $21.5 million in system sales and $1.4 million in royalties across those 250 stores. We can change the assumptions to see how much the case depends on them.

These are sales and receipts, not incremental profit. Franchisee payback would require a separate cash-flow view covering initial investment, operating margins, carrying costs, and the cost of the solution. And four months of acceleration is an assumption to test, not an established opportunity.

I would use this model to ask how many openings we can influence and how much time we can credibly save.

Slide 06 / 12 · The CEO conversation

Ask the CEO to sponsor a joint assessment of opening delays and their financial impact

Ask for a focused assessment with a clear decision at day 30.

I would bring a hypothesis to the CEO and use the meeting to test whether it addresses a real priority.

First, where does growth actually stall? Which openings slip, why do they slip, and is opening speed more valuable right now than improving profit in existing stores?

Second, what is already working? I’d want to understand the tools, partners, and improvement programs already in place, and where people still spend time chasing documents or decisions.

Third, what evidence would justify investment? We should agree on the time and cost outcomes that matter, the readiness standards we cannot compromise, and the level of confidence needed to proceed.

My specific ask would be sponsorship for a scoped 30-day assessment, access to the relevant teams and project records, and a weekly working review. At day 30, we would decide whether to pilot, change focus, or stop.

Slide 07 / 12 · The first 30 days

Use the first 30 days to find the delays and choose what to build first

Establish the baseline, test real examples, and select a bounded pilot.

I’d work with five to eight franchisees, review 20 to 30 recent openings, and follow five to ten stores currently being built. Those are proposed sample sizes, chosen to make the assessment manageable while exposing variation.

In the first week, we would reconstruct the process from schedules, emails, drawings, and costs, then interview the people doing the work. The goal is a reliable timeline for each opening.

In the second week, we would identify the causes of lost time and cost. We need to separate delays we can influence from outside approvals, and avoid adding together delays that overlap.

Then we would test AI on real documents and questions, including projects held out from the prototype work. I’d want a record of errors as well as a good demo: wrong versions, missed requirements, unsupported answers, and incorrect routing.

By day 30, we should have a prioritized workflow, a baseline, an accountable owner, a pilot budget, and agreed success measures. If the preventable delays are too small or the data is unusable, we should change direction.

Slide 08 / 12 · Days 30–90

Over days 30–90, we will build and pilot an AI workspace that keeps openings moving

Pilot three connected workflows inside the team’s daily work.

The first product is an opening workspace connected to the records teams already use: emails, contractor updates, drawings, permit comments, schedules, and checklists.

I would start with three workflows. First, prepare a permit filing by checking completeness and drafting responses. Second, answer contractor questions using the latest approved information, with links back to the source. Third, update the opening plan when a delivery date or other dependency changes.

Days 31 to 45 are about connecting systems and defining those workflows. Days 46 to 60 are about building the workspace and automations. Days 61 to 90 are about using them on five to ten live openings and improving the product from daily feedback.

Each workflow needs a named business owner, source permissions, and clear rules for when a person must review an action. Ode would bring product and engineering delivery; the client team would bring process ownership and access to operators.

We would measure resubmissions, response time, days blocked, usage, and errors. A 90-day pilot may not capture the full opening cycle, so I would report early workflow results separately from later lease-to-open and financial outcomes. Comparisons should account for differences in project type and jurisdiction.

Slide 09 / 12 · The transformation roadmap

Beyond reducing payback periods by making openings more efficient, AI has the potential to change how every franchise operates

Use the opening workflow as a foundation for broader operating improvements.

The longer-term vision is to give every franchisee access to the knowledge of the whole system.

Before a lease is signed, that could mean better site underwriting: estimating sales, build-out costs, and the effect on nearby stores. Inside the store, it could mean using sales, weather, events, and catering demand to improve ordering and preparation. Across the network, it could mean a support partner that understands each operator’s stores and helps resolve recurring issues.

Each opportunity needs its own baseline and outcome measure: forecast error and investment return for site selection, waste and stockouts for demand planning, and resolution time and operator hours for support.

Jersey Mike’s already has technology supporting parts of these workflows. The next step would be to understand the remaining gaps and build on those investments.

The opening workspace is the entry point. Expansion should follow demonstrated value, adoption, and a clear business case for the next workflow.

Slide 10 / 12 · The Blackstone opportunity

Jersey Mike’s case study can help Ode bring AI transformation to other Blackstone restaurant businesses

Turn a measured result into a credible introduction and a repeatable delivery approach.

If the Jersey Mike’s pilot produces a credible result, it becomes a concrete case study for conversations with other Blackstone restaurant businesses.

Tropical Smoothie Cafe and 7 Brew are examples in the deck. Both have development activity where coordination across permits, utilities, equipment, and opening teams may matter. But their operating models and physical formats differ, so the same solution would need adaptation.

I would bring evidence on days saved, operator adoption, errors, and the cost to deliver and support the product. That gives a development or operations leader something specific to evaluate.

Blackstone relationships could help secure introductions, but each company still needs its own sponsor, business case, data access, and purchasing decision.

What should become repeatable is the assessment process, the common integrations, the workflow components, and the measurement approach. That is how a successful engagement could make the next one faster and more economical to deliver.

Slide 11 / 12 · Beyond Blackstone

Restaurant transformation can become a repeatable business across dozens of enterprise clients

A focused restaurant capability can serve several distinct buyer groups.

The deck identifies 36 prospect organizations across three groups: 15 public restaurant companies, 18 private chains and sponsor-backed groups, and three large multi-unit franchise operators.

These are named prospects, not qualified pipeline or a total market-size estimate. The wider set of 100 brands in the QSR reports is a prospecting pool; multiple brands can share one corporate buyer.

The sales approach would differ by segment. For public companies, I’d look for development and operations leaders with a measurable priority. For sponsor-backed groups, I’d use sponsor and operator relationships to find the right entry point. For large franchise operators, one relationship could potentially support work across several brands.

I would prioritize accounts with repeated openings, material coordination problems, accessible records, and an executive willing to own the outcome.

The commercial thesis is that Ode can develop a repeatable restaurant transformation capability, supported by measured client results and improving delivery economics. The first engagement has to establish that foundation.

Start with one valuable workflow, prove the result, and earn the right to expand.

My core thesis is that AI can help franchisees open sooner and operate more profitably by reducing the coordination work that slows them down.

I would start with a focused assessment of opening execution, build only where the evidence supports it, and use a live pilot to establish whether the product changes outcomes. A successful result could then support expansion across the franchise and into other restaurant businesses.

Thank you. I’d welcome your questions, especially on where you would pressure-test the thesis or narrow the first pilot.
